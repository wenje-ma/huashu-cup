Abstract
With the explosive growth in demand for artificial intelligence and computing power,
the energy consumption of data centers has sharply increased, making their green and
low-carbon operation an urgent need. Shared energy storage (SES), as a flexible regulation
resource, can effectively facilitate renewable energy consumption and reduce system costs.
However, existing research mostly regards data center loads as rigid loads, ignoring the
elastic scheduling potential of latency-insensitive computing tasks, and lacking a game
decision-making model from the perspective of SES operators to provide strategies for
data center SES transactions. Therefore, this study constructs a SES trading optimization
model that takes into account the flexibility of computing power. The upper-level targets
profit maximization for the SES operator by optimizing charge and discharge strategies and
service pricing, while the lower level minimizes the total energy cost of each computing
center by jointly optimizing computing task scheduling and energy storage utilization
plans. Finally, a bi-level Stackelberg game-based optimization model is constructed to solve
the SES dispatch and, achieve benefit coordination between SES and computing center.
The simulation results indicate that using SES without implementing load shifting cannot
optimize the total cost of the computing power center, offering only storage revenue. Only
by integrating SES with load shifting can a significant cost reduction be realized. The
model presented in this article resulted in an SES revenue of 2605.34 yuan and a 3.14%
reduction in computing center costs. This study provides a theoretical basis and decision-
making support for shared energy storage participation in the computing power market
and contributes to accelerating the coordinated development of computing power and
electricity systems.

power industry has undergone explosive growth, giving rise to non-negligible energy

consumption challenges. Taking data centers as an example, national electricity consump-
tion by data centers exceeded 150 billion kWh in 2023, accounting for 1.6% of total social
electricity consumption, with the growth rate of energy demand becoming increasingly
prominent. Under the explosive growth of computing power demand, the annual electricity
consumption of data centers across the country is rapidly increasing. The traditional power
supply mode that relies on long-distance transmission from the large power grid and
significant peak–valley fluctuations not only fails to meet the rigid constraints of intelli-
gent computing clusters on uninterrupted and highly reliable power, but also increases
carbon emission intensity due to the high proportion of thermal power. In this context,
it is urgently necessary to explore novel models including proximity renewable energy
supply, aggregated trading, and on-site consumption, so as to increase the proportion of
green electricity in data center operations, promote efficient green power-driven computing
loads, and achieve safe, reliable, low-carbon, and economical operation objectives, thereby
supporting high-quality development of data centers.
As a flexible regulation resource, shared energy storage demonstrates significant value
in promoting renewable energy accommodation and reducing overall system costs. On
the one hand, energy storage systems (ESS) can charge during off-peak electricity price
periods and discharge during peak periods, effectively mitigating the peak–valley load
difference and alleviating grid congestion. This arbitrage capability, coupled with the pro-
vision of ancillary services such as frequency regulation, enhances the economic viability
of storage assets. On the other hand, data centers, as high-energy-consuming power users,
can leverage energy storage to increase their proportion of green electricity access, thereby
accelerating their green and low-carbon transition. By integrating ESS with power purchase
agreements for renewable energy, data centers can shift flexible loads to periods of abun-
dant renewable generation, enabling higher utilization of green electricity. Furthermore,
shared energy storage enables multiple data centers within a cluster to collectively benefit
from economies of scale, reducing individual investment costs and improving overall re-
source utilization efficiency. Preliminary studies have been conducted on the collaborative
optimization of data centers and shared energy storage. Jiang et al. proposed a two-stage,
bi-level collaborative framework to optimize the capacity planning of energy storage sys-
tems for computing centers under the direct green power supply mode, considering both
investment and operational decisions [ 1]. Shared energy storage dispatch optimization
models for data center clusters demonstrate the benefits of coordinated scheduling among
multiple facilities [2,3]. These works largely concentrate on dispatch strategies and capacity
configuration under deterministic or stochastic frameworks. However, the interplay be-
tween computing task scheduling flexibility and pricing mechanisms, particularly within a
game-theoretic context, has not been fully addressed, leaving room for further investigation
into the strategic interactions between shared energy storage operators and data center
users in electricity markets.
Data centers can not only actively participate in demand response from the power
side through energy storage systems, but also possess regulatory potential from the com-
puting side. This computing-side flexibility arises from the temporal shiftability of specific
workloads, which can be rescheduled to periods of lower electricity prices or higher re-
newable energy availability, complementing the physical flexibility provided by storage.
Data center workloads can typically be classified into two categories: latency-sensitive
tasks and latency-tolerant tasks. Latency-sensitive tasks, such as model training, offline
inference, and batch processing jobs, only need to be completed before their deadlines,
with processing time that can be flexibly scheduled within a certain window, exhibiting
significant temporal flexibility [ 4]. This elasticity allows data centers to shift electricity
consumption across time without violating service level agreements, creating new op-

for computing-power coordination, they predominantly assume dedicated storage assets
and do not investigate the strategic pricing interactions between a shared energy storage
operator and multiple data center users. This gap highlights the need for a game-theoretic
model that integrates computing flexibility into shared energy storage trading, which is the
focus of this work.
A mathematical framework for analyzing strategic interactions among multiple
decision-makers, game theory offers a systematic approach to multi-agent optimization
problems characterized by conflicting or partially aligned interests. Unlike conventional
centralized optimization methods, game theory does not require the assumption of com-
plete information sharing or perfectly aligned objectives among participants. Instead, by
explicitly modeling the decision variables, strategy spaces, and payoff functions of each
agent, it seeks equilibrium solutions in which no participant has an incentive to unilaterally
deviate from their chosen strategy—thereby more realistically capturing the decentralized,
self-interested decision-making behavior observed in real-world systems. Among the
various game-theoretic frameworks, the Stackelberg leader–follower game has attracted
particular attention for its ability to capture the inherent hierarchical relationship that exists
among decision-makers, in which the leader formulates its strategy by anticipating the
follower’s optimal response, while the follower subsequently reacts optimally upon observ-
ing the leader’s decision, together forming a bi-level optimization problem. This modeling
paradigm has been widely applied across domains such as resource allocation, dynamic
pricing, and task scheduling to address multi-agent coordination problems characterized
by information asymmetry and unequal decision-making power, offering a theoretical foun-
dation for designing operational mechanisms that simultaneously safeguard the economic
interests of individual participants and improve overall system efficiency.
Research in the energy market typically involves numerous stakeholders, such as
power grids, consumers, and energy storage operators. Their interactions in power genera-
tion, transmission, trading, and consumption are often both competitive and cooperative,
which is naturally suitable for game theory methods. In recent years, game theory has
been widely applied to model typical interactions in electricity markets, for instance,
pricing games between electricity retailers and consumers over time-of-use tariffs, and
non-cooperative games among generators bidding for grid access. In shared energy stor-
age transactions among data centers, the energy storage operator acts as the price setter,
while individual data centers respond by scheduling their flexible loads and storage usage,
naturally forming a Stackelberg game. The absence of such a pricing interaction model
means that the mechanism for balancing storage revenue against data centers’ energy
costs remains underexplored, thereby limiting the practical applicability of shared storage
solutions in emerging computing power markets.
Therefore, this paper takes shared energy storage as the research object and fully
considers the elastic scheduling characteristics of latency-insensitive batch processing tasks
within data centers. We constructed a Stackelberg game optimization model for shared
energy storage trading that explicitly accounts for computing flexibility. The upper level

aims to maximize the profit of the shared energy storage operator by jointly optimizing
the charging and discharging strategies of the energy storage system and the service

This work provides a theoretical basis and decision support for shared energy storage
participation in the computing market, facilitating the efficient integration of computing
flexibility and storage sharing toward cost-effective and low-carbon data center operations.
2. Literature Review
Compared with centralized energy storage, shared energy storage demonstrates signif-
icant advantages in utilization rate, cost sharing, and dispatch flexibility. Li et al. conducted
an empirical comparison in residential scenarios and found that the annual utilization rate
of energy storage devices can be improved under the shared model, while the energy cost
per user is reduced, providing direct economic evidence for the promotion of shared energy
storage [7]. Miller and Carriveau proposed that the synergy between shared energy storage
dispatch strategies and market mechanisms is a key factor affecting the development of
energy storage [ 8]. Xie et al. investigated the potential advantages of the multi-agent
distributed shared energy storage service pattern in distribution networks [9]. Xiao et al.
proposed an online coordinated dispatch framework for shared energy storage in intercon-
nected data center microgrid scenarios, achieving energy complementarity among multiple
microgrids [9]. For the business model, Kang et al. further proposed a two-stage multi-load
collaborative shared energy storage model, realizing demand response optimization for
multiple types of loads in data center clusters [10].
In the study of trading pricing mechanisms, game theory is the dominant approach.
Jiang et al. adopted a cooperative game theory perspective and utilized the Shapley value
for revenue allocation, proving the applicability of cooperative game theory in multi-
microgrid shared energy storage scenarios [11]. Xu et al. proposed a bi-level optimization
model for the shared hybrid hydrogen energy storage system to optimize the capacity
configuration decisions and the pricing strategy jointly [12]. Li et al. proposed a stochastic
optimized configuration method of SES in renewable energy power plants considering
multiple primary–secondary game-pricing modes based on the uncertainty of renewable
energy output [13]. However, most of the aforementioned trading models assume user
loads as rigid demands and do not fully consider the dispatch potential of adjustable
resources on the user side.
As high-energy-consumption entities, data centers exert a significant influence on the
dispatch strategies of shared energy storage through their load characteristics. Although
conventional studies typically treat data centers as rigid electricity consumers, recent re-
search has begun to systematically quantify their flexibility resources. In recent years,
research on computing-energy synergy has progressively deepened. Su et al. leveraged
IoT technology to quantify the adjustable potential of data centers, providing data sup-
port for elastic computing scheduling [14]. Lu et al. proposed an online elastic resource
provisioning framework with QoS guarantees, and theoretically proved that computing
resources in container-based cloud scenarios can be elastically scheduled online while
satisfying quality-of-service constraints [15]. Zhang et al. further systematically unlocked

data center flexibility by proposing a joint optimization method for energy storage dispatch
and design under progressive loading, quantifying the regulation capacity that data centers
can provide to the grid [16].
From the perspective of computing-energy collaborative optimization, Liu et al. jointly
modeled the spatiotemporal migration characteristics of data center computing loads with
shared energy storage dispatch, achieving computing-energy synergy optimization from a
virtual power plant perspective [17]. Wu investigated the configuration and operation of
data center shared energy storage from a stochastic programming perspective, considering
the dual uncertainties of renewable generation and computing demand [ 18]. However,
most existing studies on computing flexibility focus on internal optimization of individual
data centers. Few works have incorporated computing elasticity into the shared energy
storage trading framework, leaving the potential value of “computing + storage” synergy
insufficiently explored.
The Stackelberg game is capable of characterizing the hierarchical decision-making
relationship between leaders and followers in the market, and has been widely applied
in energy-trading modeling. Its core advantage lies in the rational allocation of decision-
making authority: the upper-level leader first sets prices or strategies, while the lower-level
followers optimize their own decisions under the given strategies, with the equilibrium
ultimately solved through backward induction. In the field of shared energy storage, Wang
et al. applied a Stackelberg game to optimize the trading strategy of shared energy storage,
proving that this method has improved economic benefits for all participants [19]. Jiang et al.
adopted a cooperative game theory perspective and utilized the Shapley value for revenue
allocation, which can serve as a methodological comparison to the bi-level game [11]. Lin
et al. established a collaborative optimization dispatch model for multiple virtual power
plants and shared energy storage considering electricity price uncertainty, employing robust
optimization to handle price fluctuations; however, service quality was not addressed [20].
The aforementioned studies have validated the effectiveness of the bi-level game in energy
storage trading across different scenarios, yet they share a common limitation: none of
them consider the impact of user-side load flexibility on trading equilibrium. In data center
scenarios, computing elasticity itself constitutes a tradable flexibility resource. Excluding it
from the game framework not only underestimates the system’s regulation potential but
also causes pricing strategies to deviate from the true equilibrium.
In addition, recent advances in intelligent optimization and machine learning meth-
ods, such as reinforcement learning based dynamic resource allocation [ 21], intelligent
optimization of energy systems under varying operating conditions [22], and intelligent
digital architectures and optimization strategies for complex industrial systems [23], which
have demonstrated significant advantages in handling high-dimensional, nonlinear, and
time-varying decision-making problems in energy management and resource scheduling.
These studies provide cross-domain methodological support for the modeling approach
adopted in this work, which embeds computational flexibility into the game equilibrium.
At the same time, they also indicate that existing game-theoretic frameworks based on de-
terministic mathematical programming still have room for improvement when addressing
dynamic, high-dimensional scenarios, an aspect identified as a direction for future research
in this work.

the collaborative optimization of data centers and shared energy storage; however, the
following limitations remain, as shown in Table 1. On one hand, the majority of studies
treat data center loads as rigid loads, failing to fully exploit the elastic scheduling potential
of latency-insensitive computing tasks. This oversight means that the temporal flexibility
inherent in tasks such as model training, offline inference, and batch processing is not

3. Problem Formulation
The operation framework of the data center and shared energy storage is illustrated
in Figure 1. As shown in the figure, the grid-distributed photovoltaic (PV) systems, dis-
tributed wind power, and energy storage station modules jointly supply electricity to the
data center. The energy storage system can serve as a flexible resource in the electricity
market by purchasing low-cost electricity during peak periods for storage and subsequently
discharging it during demand peak periods of the computing center, thereby balancing
supply and demand, reducing energy waste, and capturing revenue. In this paper, the
energy storage system is regarded as an independent market participant, equivalent to
other market entities, providing services to multiple data centers within the park under a
shared mode.
Figure 1. Data center operation framework diagram.
Although the framework above outlines the basic operational logic of the system, the
following key challenges remain in the coordinated operation of shared energy storage and
multiple data centers:
(1) Interest coordination and gaming challenge. The energy storage operator aims to
maximize arbitrage revenue, while each data center aims to minimize its electricity
procurement cost. These objectives are conflicting yet interdependent, making it
necessary to establish an effective coordination mechanism.
(2) Heterogeneous load-scheduling challenge. Data centers contain both latency-sensitive
tasks and shiftable data loads (SDL). Without a unified pricing signal, coordinating
load scheduling across multiple data centers to achieve system-wide peak-shaving
and valley-filling remains a systematic challenge.
(3) Absence of a dynamic pricing mechanism. Existing studies often treat the storage
operator’s pricing decisions and the data centers’ scheduling responses separately,
https://doi.org/10.3390/en19153681

--- Page 8 ---

lacking a bi-level dynamic mechanism that captures their strategic interaction, making
it difficult to characterize the system’s equilibrium under multi-agent gaming.
To address the above challenges, this paper aims to:
• Establish a Stackelberg game-based bi-level decision-making framework, in which the
energy storage operator acts as the leader setting the internal transaction price, and
the data centers act as followers optimizing their load-scheduling strategies, thereby
addressing the interest coordination challenge.
• Develop a shiftable load-scheduling optimization model for data centers that distin-
guishes between latency-sensitive and latency-tolerant tasks, enabling coordinated
load scheduling driven by the pricing signal, thereby addressing the heterogeneous
load-scheduling challenge.
• Derive and analyze the Stackelberg equilibrium of the proposed game, demonstrat-
ing that the equilibrium pricing and scheduling strategies can jointly optimize stor-
age revenue and data center costs, thereby addressing the absence of a dynamic
pricing mechanism.
4. Methodology
Based on the aforementioned problem description and operational framework, this pa-
per constructs a bi-level decision-making model for the collaborative optimization between
the shared energy storage (SES) operator and data centers, aiming to achieve coordinated
optimal dispatch of shared energy storage and computing resources. In this bi-level
decision-making model, the SES operator serves as the upper-level leader, while the data
centers act as lower-level followers.
4.1. Upper-Level Decision Model
The upper-level decision model maximizes the profit of the shared energy storage (SES)
operator by transmitting electricity price signals and demand response instructions to the
lower-level data centers, thereby incentivizing the data centers to adjust their computing
loads across different time periods and guiding them to optimize their own electricity
consumption decisions.

5. Case Study
5.1. Basic Data and Parameters
To validate the proposed model and algorithm, simulation experiments are conducted
based on the scale of a real-world data center, with partial parameters adopted from [33,34].
Three data centers (IDC1, IDC2, IDC3), constructed and managed by the same operator, are
considered. The three data centers are geographically adjacent and share identical hardware
components and performance parameters across their servers. The scheduling time step
is set to 1 h, with a unit-scheduling horizon of 1 day. Data center energy consumption
parameters are referenced from [17]. The shared energy storage system constructed in this
paper employs lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries as the storage medium. The shared energy
storage system has a total power capacity of 1000 kW and an energy capacity of 6000 kWh.
The state of charge (SOC) is constrained within [0.1, 0.9], with an initial SOC of 0.5. The
self-discharge coefficient is set to 0.04% per time period 28. Both charging and discharging

5.2. Result Analysis
Based on the data center parameters and SES configurations specified above, the
optimization yields the SES charging and discharging trading strategy that accounts for
computing flexibility. The results are illustrated in Figure 2. As shown in Figure 2, the
SES exhibits two typical charge and discharge cycles. The first cycle occurs approximately
during periods 3 to 11, during which the SES starts with a low-power charging at time 3,
then rapidly charges at full power at time 4 until reaching the upper limit, and discharges
to the data center during periods 7 and 11. The second cycle occurs approximately during
periods 14 to 21, during which the ESS undergoes full-power charging again from periods
14 to 18, and discharges power during periods 19 and 21. It can be observed that the
charging of the SES is concentrated during valley and flat electricity price periods, while
discharging occurs during peak price periods. This enables the SES operator to capture
arbitrage profits by purchasing electricity at low prices and selling it at high prices.
Figure 2. Charging and discharging power of SES.
Figure 3 illustrates the daily variation curve of SOC for ESS. The SES primarily executes
a single valley-charging and peak-discharging operation. The SOC remains within the safe
operating range of [0.1, 0.9] throughout the entire day. Specifically, the SOC maintains
its initial level of approximately 0.5 during periods 0–3. During periods 4–7, the SOC
rises rapidly to the upper limit of 0.9 under high-power charging. Subsequently, the SOC
declines to approximately 0.1 due to continuous discharging. A second charging cycle is
initiated in period 13, during which the SOC recovers to approximately 0.9. Thereafter,
the SOC gradually decreases to approximately 0.48 and stabilizes during periods 20–23.
Owing to the coordination of flexible computing tasks, the SES does not need to shoulder
the entire peak-shaving burden alone. The depth of discharge cycles is moderately reduced,
which effectively mitigates electrochemical degradation of the battery and contributes to
extending its economic lifetime.

6. Conclusions
This paper addresses the shared energy storage (SES) trading optimization problem
for data center clusters, aiming to exploit the temporal flexibility of latency-insensitive
computing tasks. A bi-level Stackelberg game-based optimization model that accounts for
computing workload flexibility is proposed. The principal findings are summarized below.
First, a bi-level optimization model incorporating computing load flexibility is proposed.
The upper-level model maximizes the SES operator’s profit by optimizing the charging
and discharging strategy and internal electricity pricing. The lower-level model minimizes
the comprehensive energy cost of each data center by coordinating the scheduling of
latency-insensitive computing tasks with the SES power procurement plan.
Second, the temporal scheduling of shiftable data load demonstrates a pronounced
peak-shaving effect. By proactively migrating latency-insensitive tasks, such as model
training and offline inference, during valley price periods, data centers effectively reduce
rigid electricity demand during peak periods, thereby decreasing the volume of high-price
electricity purchased directly from the grid and improving the overall energy cost efficiency
of the system.
Third, the bi-level pricing mechanism achieves interest coordination between the SES
operator and the data center users. Through internal electricity pricing, the SES offers
discounted rates below the grid price during peak periods to incentivize data centers to pur-
chase from the SES, while encouraging flexible charging during valley periods. This ensures
the SES operator’s arbitrage margin while providing data centers with economic incentives
to participate in demand response, forming a benign market interaction mechanism.
In summary, the proposed computing-electricity coordinated bi-level optimization
model effectively integrates the regulation capability of shared energy storage with the tem-
poral flexibility of computing workloads, demonstrating significant advantages in reducing
data center comprehensive energy costs, enhancing SES operational revenue, and promot-
ing green energy consumption. Future work may further extend the current deterministic
bi-level framework toward a stochastic or robust bi-level formulation, potentially informed
by machine-learning-based approaches discussed above for handling high-dimensional
uncertainty, including renewable generation forecasting errors. In addition, incorporating
more detailed server-level operational constraints, such as server utilization limits, commu-
nication overhead and delays, task migration costs, and SLA violation penalties, would
further refine the computing-side flexibility model. Battery degradation effects and more
realistic, time-varying workload representations could also be incorporated to improve the
fidelity of the proposed framework.
