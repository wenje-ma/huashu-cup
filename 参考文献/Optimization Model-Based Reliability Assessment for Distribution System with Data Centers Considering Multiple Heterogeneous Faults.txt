Abstract
The integration of data centers (DCs) into distribution systems introduces new challenges
for reliability assessment. It also provides operational flexibility through the spatial migra-
tion of computational workloads. However, existing reliability assessment methods have
not sufficiently captured the joint effects of DC workload flexibility, post-fault network re-
configuration, and multiple heterogeneous faults. To address this gap, this paper proposes
an optimization model-based reliability assessment framework for distribution systems
with integrated DCs. A DC workload dispatch model is first developed by considering
server operation, cooling demand, backup requirements, and inter-DC workload transfer
limits. The post-fault operation process is then formulated as a two-stage optimization
problem, including fault isolation and service restoration. The proposed framework co-
optimizes network reconfiguration and DC workload migration under line faults, node
faults, and remote-controlled switch failures. The resulting problem is reformulated as
a mixed-integer linear programming model and solved using commercial solvers. Case
studies on a modified IEEE 123-node test feeder show that DC workload migration can
significantly reduce expected energy not supplied by exploiting the spatial flexibility of
interconnected DCs. The results also demonstrate that neglecting node faults and switch
failures may lead to overly optimistic reliability estimates. Additional tests on a modified
356-node regional distribution system further verify the applicability and scalability of the
proposed framework. The proposed method provides a physically interpretable reliability
assessment tool for active distribution systems with DC integration and heterogeneous
fault mechanisms.
Keywords: data center workload migration; distribution system reliability assessment;
heterogeneous faults; network reconfiguration; service restoration
1. Introduction
The rapid proliferation of the Internet of Things, artificial intelligence, and cloud com-
puting has accelerated the expansion of data centers (DCs) as the primary computational
backbone [1,2]. However, the continuous operation and intensive energy consumption
of DCs pose significant challenges to distribution networks. In 2021, the total electricity

consumption of DCs in China reached 216.6 TWh, representing approximately 2.99% of the
national total demand [3,4]. This figure is projected to exceed 700 TWh by 2035. As critical
infrastructure for digital transformation, DCs support essential sectors such as healthcare
and transportation [5,6]. Consequently, power supply interruptions triggered by distri-
bution network contingencies directly jeopardize the operational security of DCs. Such
outages entail substantial socio-economic losses and potentially catastrophic systemic risks.
Therefore, it is imperative to develop precise reliability assessment models for distribution
systems that explicitly account for DC integration.
DCs are characterized by intensive power demand, concentrated spatial distribution,
and highly controllable operational attributes. Crucially, DCs possess significant energy
flexibility [7]. Leveraging cloud interconnections and data center networks, interconnected
DCs enable the spatial migration of computational workloads. This capability effectively
reconfigures the power load distribution across the distribution network. In the event
of network contingencies that threaten DC operations, computational tasks can be trans-
ferred to unaffected sites [8,9]. Such proactive load redistribution enhances overall system
reliability and ensures the secure operation of the power grid.
Distribution system reliability assessment has been extensively studied. Existing
methods can be broadly classified into analytical methods, Monte Carlo simulations, and
optimization-based approaches. Analytical methods are computationally efficient and
can provide closed-form or semi-analytical reliability indices [ 10–12]. They are suitable
for systems with relatively fixed topology, predefined restoration rules, and limited un-
certainty representation. Monte Carlo simulation is more flexible in modeling stochastic
component failures, repair processes, renewable generation uncertainty, and time-varying
operating conditions [13–15]. It should be noted that modern Monte Carlo-based reliability
assessment is not limited to purely heuristic restoration logic. Some recent implemen-
tations incorporate optimization-based restoration models to better represent post-fault
operational decisions [16]. Nevertheless, analytical and simulation-based methods may
still face limitations when complex restoration actions, multiple flexible resources, and
cyber-physical coupling need to be represented explicitly.
To address these limitations, optimization-based reliability assessment has attracted
increasing attention in recent years [17–21]. These methods embed post-fault restoration
decisions into explicit mathematical programming models. For example, topology-variable-
based linear formulations have been developed to evaluate distribution system reliability
while considering switching interruptions [17]. MILP-based models have also been proposed
for reliability-constrained distribution network planning and expansion, allowing reliability
indices to be directly incorporated into planning decisions [ 18,19]. More recent studies
have further extended this line of research. Li et al. developed a linear-programming-
based reliability assessment method that explicitly considers distribution automation and
distributed generation [22]. Zhang et al. proposed a generalized MILP model for reliability
evaluation of cabled active distribution networks with multiple devices [23]. In addition, cyber-
physical factors and recovery resource decisions have been incorporated into recent reliability
assessment frameworks for cyber-physical distribution systems [24]. These optimization-based
approaches can explicitly represent network reconfiguration, load shedding, and service
restoration decisions. Therefore, they provide a rigorous framework for quantifying the
reliability contribution of post-fault operational flexibility .
Despite these advances, existing optimization-based reliability studies still have sev-
eral limitations when applied to active distribution systems with integrated DCs. First,
most existing models focus on physical-network-side resources, such as switches, distribu-
tion automation devices, distributed generators, and energy storage systems. These models
can explicitly represent network reconfiguration, load shedding, and service restoration.

However, they do not fully capture the operational flexibility provided by interconnected
DCs. In particular, the spatial migration of computational workloads can change the ef-
fective electrical demand at different locations after faults. This cyber-layer flexibility is
different from conventional load shedding or local service restoration.
Second, recent studies on cyber-physical reliability and machine-learning-assisted
reliability assessment have highlighted the importance of communication systems, data-
driven prediction, and intelligent restoration decisions [25,26]. These studies provide useful
tools for modeling cyber-physical interactions and improving computational efficiency.
Nevertheless, the coordination between cyber-layer computational resource scheduling
and physical-layer distribution network restoration remains insufficiently represented in
explicit reliability optimization models. As a result, the reliability contribution of DC
workload migration cannot be directly quantified by existing frameworks.
Third, many distribution system reliability studies still represent contingencies mainly
through line outages or simplified component-failure assumptions. Such simplifications
are suitable for conventional reliability studies, but they may be insufficient for modern
active distribution systems with extensive automation devices. In practice, node faults
and remote-controlled switch failures may change the feasible isolation actions, reduce the
available restoration paths, and enlarge the outage area. Ignoring these heterogeneous fault
mechanisms may therefore lead to overly optimistic reliability estimates.
To address the above research gaps, this paper proposes an optimization model-based
reliability assessment framework for distribution systems with integrated DCs. The proposed
framework is not intended to replace existing analytical, Monte Carlo-based, or optimization-
based reliability assessment methods. Instead, it extends the optimization-based reliability
assessment paradigm to a new cyber-physical operating context in which DC workload
migration interacts with post-fault network restoration. The key distinction of this work lies in
the unified modeling of three coupled factors: physical-layer network reconfiguration, cyber-
layer DC workload migration, and multiple heterogeneous fault mechanisms. Under the
assumptions of linearized distribution power flow, equivalent DC workload-transfer capacity ,
and MILP-representable post-fault operation constraints, the proposed model evaluates
reliability by explicitly optimizing the fault isolation and service restoration processes. This
enables the reliability contribution of DC spatial flexibility and the risk impact of node and
switch failures to be quantified within the same mathematical framework.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
1. An optimization model-based reliability assessment framework is developed for active
distribution systems with integrated DCs. Different from conventional reliability
assessment models that mainly evaluate physical load restoration, the proposed
framework incorporates DC workload migration into post-fault reliability evaluation.
This allows the cyber-layer flexibility of interconnected DCs to be quantified in terms
of its impact on expected energy not supplied.
2. Multiple heterogeneous faults are explicitly incorporated into the reliability assess-
ment framework, including line faults, node faults, and remote-controlled switch
failures. This extends conventional line-fault-oriented reliability evaluation and en-
ables a more realistic assessment of how different component failures affect fault
isolation, service restoration, network topology, and reliability indices.
3. The proposed reliability assessment problem is reformulated as an MILP model and
tested on modified IEEE 123-node and 356-node distribution systems. The case
studies quantify the marginal contribution of DC workload migration, the assessment
bias caused by neglecting heterogeneous faults, and the scalability of the proposed
formulation. These results provide practical insights for reliability-oriented planning
and post-fault operation of active distribution systems with DC integration.

3. Optimization Model-Based Reliability Assessment
In this section, an optimization model-based reliability assessment method is devel-
oped for active distribution systems with multiple resources. The model explicitly considers
the integration of DCs and distributed generators (DGs). In particular, it incorporates the
DC workload dispatch model proposed in Section 2 to capture workload migration under
fault conditions. In addition, the proposed method accounts for multiple heterogeneous
faults, including failures of distribution lines, substations, and switches.
To achieve a more accurate reliability assessment, the post-fault operation process of the
distribution system is divided into two stages: fault isolation (FI) and service restoration (SR).
Figure 2 illustrates a typical post-fault operation process. During normal operation (NO), a
fault occurs on branch 3–4. In the FI stage, branches 3–4 and 4–5 are disconnected to isolate
the fault. As a result, an island consisting of DG 8, node 4, and DC 9 is formed. Owing to the
limited capacity of DG 8, it cannot simultaneously supply DC 9 and the loads at nodes 4, 5,
and 11. Therefore, the loads at nodes 5 and 11 are interrupted, and part of the workload of
DC 9 is migrated to DC 10. In the SR stage, the switches on branches 4–5 and 5–6 are closed.
Through network reconfiguration, power supply to all loads is successfully restored.

Equation (30) enforces that branches without RCSs remain closed. Equation (31)
indicates that, during the FI stage, switches can only be opened and cannot be closed.
Equation (32) ensures that if branch ij is faulty and equipped with an RCS, and the RCS is
not faulty, the corresponding switch is opened to isolate the fault. Equation (33) indicates
that if an RCS fails, its switching status in the FI stage remains the same as that in the NO
stage. Equations (34) and (35) indicate that if branch ij is faulty and remains closed, both
terminal nodes i and j are classified into the faulted zone. Equation (36) indicates that a
node is classified as being in the faulted zone if it experiences a fault. Equations (37) and
(38) ensure that the two terminal nodes of a closed branch belong to the same zone, namely,
either the faulted zone or the non-faulted zone. Equations (39) and (40) enforce that the
two terminal nodes of a closed branch have the same energization status. Equation (41)
indicates that a node in the faulted zone must be de-energized. Equation (42) specifies that
substation nodes are always energized.

those in the FI stage. Most variables have the same physical meanings as their FI-stage
counterparts, and the superscripts FI and SR are used only to distinguish the corresponding
post-fault operation stages. Specifically, variables with the superscript FI denote the
decisions or states during the fault isolation stage, whereas variables with the superscript
SR denote the corresponding decisions or states during the service restoration stage.
The main differences between the FI and SR formulations are as follows. First, con-
straint (31) in the FI stage is not included in the SR stage because the SR stage permits
switch closing for load restoration, whereas the FI stage only allows switch opening for
fault isolation. Second, constraint (72) requires that loads energized in the FI stage remain
supplied in the SR stage. This prevents service interruption to already restored loads and
preserves restoration continuity. Moreover, constraint (72) establishes the coupling between
the FI and SR stages by linking the load energization status in the SR stage to that in the FI
stage. Therefore, any load restored during fault isolation must remain energized during
service restoration.

The computational complexity of the proposed MILP formulation mainly depends on
the network size, the number of controllable switches, the number of DCs, and the number
of fault scenarios. For each fault scenario, increasing the number of nodes and branches
introduces more power-flow variables, voltage variables, nodal energization variables, and
branch-status variables. Increasing the number of RCSs enlarges the feasible reconfigu-
ration space and adds switching-related binary decisions. Increasing the number of DCs
introduces additional workload allocation and inter-DC migration variables, especially
when workload transfer among multiple DC pairs is considered. Therefore, the solution
time generally increases with the physical network scale, the number of controllable de-
vices, and the cyber-layer workload migration dimension. Although MILP problems have
exponential worst-case complexity with respect to the number of binary variables, the
proposed reliability assessment problem is naturally separable across fault scenarios. Once
the fault state is specified, the post-fault restoration problem for each scenario can be solved
independently. This feature enables scenario-wise parallel computation and helps maintain
computational tractability for larger systems.

(1) Ablation analysis of network reconfiguration and DC workload migration
To more explicitly distinguish the contribution of network reconfiguration from that of

Case 1, only network reconfiguration is considered. Specifically, fault isolation and service
restoration are optimized through switching operations, while DC workload migration
is disabled. Therefore, each DC must be supplied locally after faults. In Case 2, the
full proposed model is considered, in which network reconfiguration and DC workload
migration are jointly optimized. Since network reconfiguration is included in both cases,
the difference between Case 1 and Case 2 directly quantifies the additional reliability benefit
provided by DC workload flexibility.
As shown in Table 1, SAIFI, SAIDI, and ASAI remain unchanged in the two cases,
whereas EENS decreases from 3.8619 to 1.3895 when DC workload migration is enabled.
This corresponds to a reduction of approximately 64.0%. Since both cases include the same
network reconfiguration model, this reduction reflects the marginal contribution of DC
workload migration under optimized post-fault switching operations.

The physical interpretation of this result is related to the different meanings of the
reliability indices. SAIFI and SAIDI mainly characterize the interruption frequency and
outage duration of physical load points. They are determined by the fault scenarios, the
fault isolation process, the service restoration topology, and the energization status of nodes.

logic, the switching sequence, or the final restored topology of the physical distribution
network. Therefore, the set of interrupted physical load points and their outage durations
remains unchanged. As a result, SAIFI and SAIDI are almost unaffected. Since ASAI is
directly related to the accumulated outage duration, it also remains unchanged.
By contrast, EENS measures the amount of unsupplied energy. It is therefore sensi-
tive not only to whether a node is energized, but also to the magnitude of the load that
remains effectively unsupplied. DC workload migration provides cyber-layer flexibility by
transferring part of the computational workload from an affected DC to other healthy DCs
with available power supply and computing capacity. In this way, even if the original DC
node is still electrically interrupted, part of its computational service can be maintained at
remote DCs. Therefore, the equivalent unsupplied DC demand is reduced, which directly
decreases EENS. This explains why DC workload migration significantly reduces EENS
while having little effect on SAIFI and SAIDI in the present case.

workload migration can never affect interruption frequency or outage duration indices. In
more strongly coupled operating conditions, workload migration may also influence SAIFI
or SAIDI. For example, if DC service continuity is explicitly counted as part of customer
interruption statistics, successful workload migration may reduce the interruption frequency

(3) Comparison with a rule-based heuristic restoration strategy
To further demonstrate the advantage of the proposed optimization-based reliabil-
ity assessment framework, a representative rule-based heuristic restoration strategy is
introduced as a benchmark. The heuristic method follows a sequential restoration logic
commonly used in conventional reliability assessment. After fault isolation, candidate
switching actions are ranked according to load priority and electrical distance to the nearest
available source. In this study, the load priority is assigned according to the amount of
restorable active load, since no differentiated customer interruption cost is specified in the
test system. The electrical distance is measured by the shortest available path length from
the candidate restorable section to an energized source, where faulted components and
unavailable switches are excluded. If several candidate switching actions have the same
ranking, the action that restores a larger amount of load is selected first; if a tie still exists,
the action with the smaller branch index is selected to ensure deterministic implementation.
The tie switches are then closed sequentially if the resulting topology satisfies radiality,
voltage, branch capacity, and DG output constraints. After each accepted switching action,
DG outputs are adjusted within their capacity limits to supply the restored loads. If
the available supply or network capacity is insufficient, the residual load shedding is
determined according to the same priority order, with lower-priority loads shed first. Once
the network topology is determined, the remaining interrupted DC workload is transferred
greedily to healthy DCs with available power supply and service capacity, subject to the
workload transfer constraints. When multiple healthy DCs are available, the receiving
DC with the largest remaining service capacity is selected first; if a tie exists, the DC with
the shorter transfer path is selected. Different from the proposed method, this heuristic
strategy does not jointly optimize network reconfiguration and DC workload migration.

more effective post-fault restoration assessment than conventional heuristic restoration
logic. The comparison also supports the necessity of formulating reliability assessment as
an explicit optimization problem, especially for active distribution systems with flexible
DC loads and heterogeneous fault conditions.

system. Compared with the modified IEEE 123-node system, the reliability indices become
worse because the larger system contains more network components, more fault scenarios,

active distribution systems.

increases from 18 to 35. Accordingly, the average solution time per scenario increases
from 1.24 s to 5.61 s. This increase is reasonable because a larger network introduces more
power flow variables, nodal energization variables, branch status variables, and switching-
related binary variables. In addition, the increased number of RCSs enlarges the feasible
reconfiguration space, which increases the complexity of the MILP solution process.

Although the solution time increases with system size, the proposed method remains
computationally tractable for the modified 356-node system. The average solution time
remains within several seconds per scenario, indicating that the proposed MILP formulation
applies to medium-scale distribution systems. The tractability is supported by three modeling
choices. First, the nonlinear distribution power-flow equations are approximated using
a linearized DistFlow formulation. Second, the radiality constraints are modeled using
continuous auxiliary variables, which avoids introducing excessive additional binary variables.
Third, the overall reliability assessment is separable across fault scenarios. Once a fault scenario
is specified, the corresponding post-fault restoration problem can be solved independently .
This structure enables scenario-wise parallel computation and can further accelerate the
overall assessment. For very large-scale systems with extensive fault scenario enumeration,
decomposition, scenario screening, and parallel implementation may be required.
5. Conclusions
This paper proposes an optimization model-based reliability assessment framework
for active distribution systems with integrated data centers (DCs). The proposed framework
co-optimizes network reconfiguration and DC workload migration during fault isolation
and service restoration. It also incorporates multiple heterogeneous faults, including line
faults, node faults, and remote-controlled switch failures. Based on a detailed DC workload
dispatch model, the reliability assessment problem is reformulated as a mixed-integer
linear programming problem.
Case studies on a modified IEEE 123-node test feeder demonstrate the effectiveness
of the proposed method. The results show that DC workload migration reduces EENS
from 3.8619 to 1.3895, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 64.0%. This indicates
that interconnected DCs can reduce unsupplied energy by reallocating computational
workloads among geographically distributed sites. The results also show that neglecting
node faults and RCS failures leads to overly optimistic reliability estimates. An additional
test on a modified 356-node regional distribution system further verifies the applicability
of the proposed framework to a larger system.
Overall, the proposed method provides a physically interpretable reliability assess-
ment tool for distribution systems with DC integration and heterogeneous fault mecha-
nisms. Future work will further consider time-varying DC workloads, differentiated load
criticality coefficients, more detailed RCS failure modes, communication delays and failures,
and decomposition or parallel optimization techniques for very large-scale systems.
Future work will focus on several directions. First, the current model can be extended
to incorporate time-varying DC workloads and more detailed workload characteristics,
such as task priorities, latency constraints, and temporal migration flexibility. In addition,
differentiated load criticality coefficients may be introduced for conventional loads, critical
loads, and DC computational workloads. This extension would allow the model to explic-
itly represent the priority trade-off between restoring local electrical demand and migrating
computational tasks under constrained restoration resources and limited communication
bandwidth. Second, more diverse, flexible resources, including energy storage systems,
demand response, and renewable generation uncertainty, can be integrated into the reliabil-
ity assessment framework. Third, the modeling of RCS failures can be further refined. In

the present study, an RCS failure is represented as the inability of the switch to change its
normal operating status. Future work may consider more realistic failure modes, including
stuck-open failures, stuck-closed failures, actuator failures, delayed switching actions, and
communication-induced operation failures. This extension would allow the reliability
impact of automation-device failures to be evaluated more comprehensively. Fourth, more
detailed information and communication system models should be incorporated to better
characterize cyber-physical coupling in modern distribution systems. In particular, com-
munication delay, communication-link outage, command transmission failure, bandwidth
degradation, and delayed or failed DC workload migration may be explicitly modeled.
Finally, to improve scalability for larger practical systems, future studies may investigate
decomposition, acceleration, parallel computing, scenario screening, or learning-assisted
optimization methods for reliability assessment under a large number of fault scenarios.
